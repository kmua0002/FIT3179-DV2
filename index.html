How nine generations transformed the Pokémon universe

Pokémon began as a Japanese role-playing video game in 1996, featuring 151 fictional creatures. These creatures are recorded in the Pokédex, an in-game encyclopaedia of Pokémon species. Each new generation marks another era of the mainline games, typically introducing new species, regions and gameplay features. From More → To Different

The Pokédex did not simply get larger. The Pokémon being added changed too.

Growth meant more than adding new species. Across generations, Pokémon gained new types, evolutionary branches and Abilities, while their battle-stat profiles also changed. These patterns reveal how the creatures themselves evolved alongside the Pokédex.

02
Types, Evolution & Statistics

Pokémon changed too

Pokémon generations brought more than new species. The type system evolved, older evolutionary families gained new members, and battle mechanics became increasingly varied. By comparing types, evolution relationships and base statistics, we can explore how Pokémon changed across nine generations.

Type System Milestones

Pokémon's type system expanded from 15 to 18 types

Every Pokémon has one or two types, such as Water, Fire or Electric. These types influence strengths and weaknesses during battles. The original games contained 15 types, including Dragon. Generation II introduced Dark and Steel, while Generation VI introduced Fairy.

1996 · GENERATION I 15 types The original type system, including Dragon.
1999 · GENERATION II 17 types Dark and Steel added, changing battle matchups.
2013 · GENERATION VI 18 types Fairy introduced, with some older Pokémon reclassified.

Why this matters: Some Pokémon changed types after their original generation. For example, Clefairy was a Normal-type Pokémon until Generation VI, when it became Fairy-type. Evolution Across Generations

Generation IV expanded older Pokémon families more than any other generation

Pokémon evolution is the process by which one Pokémon species changes into another. Species connected through evolution form an evolutionary family. While many evolutionary families are introduced together, later generations can add new evolutions or pre-evolutions to existing families. Across nine generations, 71 newly introduced species joined families from earlier generations, extending 57 existing families. Generation IV contributed the most, adding 29 species to older families, equivalent to 27.1% of its 107 newly introduced Pokémon. Evolution Case Study

Eevee's evolutionary family grew from three branches to eight

Eevee was introduced in Generation I with three possible evolutions: Vaporeon, Jolteon and Flareon. Later generations expanded the same evolutionary family. Generation II introduced Espeon and Umbreon, Generation IV added Leafeon and Glaceon, and Generation VI introduced Sylveon alongside the new Fairy type. These additions also demonstrate how evolution methods became more varied, involving friendship, time of day, special locations and affection. Evolution Case Study

One Pokémon, 63 different appearances: Alcremie's unusual evolution

While Eevee evolves into eight distinct species, Generation VIII's Alcremie demonstrates a different kind of variety. When Milcery evolves into Alcremie, the player's spinning direction, duration and time of day determine one of nine cream appearances. The Sweet held by Milcery determines one of seven decorations, creating 63 possible combinations within a single Pokémon species. Battle Mechanics

Generation III introduced nearly a quarter of all recorded Pokémon Abilities

Every Pokémon can possess an Ability, a passive trait that affects how it behaves in battle or sometimes outside it. Unlike a battle move, an Ability generally works automatically when its conditions are met. Abilities were first introduced as a game mechanic in Generation III. The original Generation I and II games did not have them, although Pokémon from those generations can possess Abilities in newer games. Generation III introduced 76 of the 314 recorded main-series Abilities, accounting for 24.2% of the dataset. Subsequent generations continued introducing new Abilities, expanding the range of available battle mechanics.

How to read: Each rectangle represents a generation from III to IX. The area of each rectangle is proportional to the number of distinct Abilities introduced in that generation. Generation III is highlighted in yellow because it introduced the Ability mechanic and contributed the largest number of new Abilities. Generations I and II do not appear because Abilities did not yet exist as a game mechanic. These counts include main-series Abilities recorded in the PokéAPI dataset snapshot through Generation IX. Hidden Abilities, introduced in Generation V, are alternative Ability assignments rather than an entirely separate set of Ability names.

Games & Global Reach

The games evolved alongside Pokémon

As Pokémon expanded across generations, its mainline RPG adventures moved between successive Nintendo consoles. Alongside new releases, remakes revisited older adventures, while international release delays gradually shortened. Together, these changes transformed how Pokémon games were developed, revisited and experienced around the world.

Beyond the Original RPG

Pokémon grew into manga, cards, anime and retail within its first years

Pokémon began as a Japanese role-playing video game in February 1996. The franchise quickly expanded beyond its original games. Manga and trading cards appeared during 1996, followed by television animation in 1997 and the first Pokémon Center Tokyo store in 1998. Over the following decades, new genres and platforms introduced further ways to experience Pokémon, from Mystery Dungeon and Pokémon GO to TCG Pocket and Pokopia. Trading Card Game

Pokémon card expansions followed different release rhythms in Japan and English markets

The Pokémon Trading Card Game (TCG) is a collectible card game in which players build decks using Pokémon, Trainer and Energy cards. New cards are regularly introduced through expansions, which are named collections of cards released as part of the game's ongoing development. Expansion sets are not the same as individual booster packs. Multiple sets can also launch on the same date. Between 2017 and 2025, our provisional catalogue records 50 distinct English launch dates and 75 Japanese launch dates. Compare the calendars to explore how frequently new expansions appeared in each market.

Pokémon in the Real World

Pokémon stepped into the real world

Pokémon's expansion also extends beyond screens, cards and retail products into physical public spaces. In Japan, Poké Lids are specially designed utility-hole covers featuring Pokémon artwork in different communities. Using a snapshot of 470 recorded installations across 41 prefectures, we explore where these designs appear, how they are distributed, and how prominently the original Generation I Pokémon are represented.

Prefecture Comparison

Four prefectures account for over a third of the recorded Poké Lids

The 470 Poké Lids are not evenly distributed across Japan. Hokkaido leads with 50 recorded installations, followed by Fukushima (43), Miyagi (37) and Iwate (36). Together, these four prefectures account for 35.3% of the dataset.

How to read: Each circle represents one prefecture, with its area proportional to the number of recorded Poké Lids. Circles are positioned at a representative recorded Poké Lid location within each prefecture. Prefectures without records in this dataset are not shown with a circle.

Generation I Footprint

The original 151 feature on two-thirds of recorded Poké Lids

Despite making up only 14.7% of the nine-generation Pokédex, Generation I Pokémon feature on 315 of the 470 recorded Poké Lids (67.0%). Their representation varies considerably across Japan. All 50 recorded Poké Lids in Hokkaido feature at least one Generation I Pokémon, compared with just 4 of 31 in Mie Prefecture.

WHAT THIS TELLS US

Generation I remains highly visible in this public-art dataset. However, the presence of older Pokémon on Poké Lids reflects the designs selected for these installations. It does not, by itself, prove that Generation I Pokémon are more popular among players.

How to read: Darker prefectures have a higher percentage of recorded Poké Lids featuring at least one Generation I Pokémon. Each lid counts once, even if it features multiple Generation I Pokémon. Grey prefectures have no records in this dataset snapshot, while pale blue can represent a genuine 0%. Percentages are based on the 470-record snapshot, not every Poké Lid currently installed in Japan.

The Bigger Picture

From 151 Pokémon to a worldwide phenomenon

Across nine generations, Pokémon expanded from 151 to 1,025 species, but its transformation went far beyond adding new creatures. New types, evolving families and Abilities changed how Pokémon could be played. The games moved across Nintendo consoles, revisited familiar adventures through remakes, and progressed towards coordinated international releases. Beyond the mainline RPG, trading cards, animation and spin-off games created new ways to experience the franchise. Pokémon also became part of physical public spaces: in our Japanese Poké Lid dataset, 315 of 470 installations feature at least one original Generation I Pokémon. Pokémon's story is not just one of growth, but of continual reinvention while maintaining a connection to its beginnings.

How the data were prepared

Pokédex and battle mechanics
The analysis covers the 1,025 species introduced in Generations I–IX, rather than treating alternate forms as additional species. Types and statistics reflect the collected modern data, not reconstructed historical game versions. Evolution relationships and Ability counts were derived from PokéAPI records.
Games and wider franchise
Related paired game versions count as one game family. Console charts use first Japanese releases, not ports or the entire commercial lifespan of each console. The franchise timeline is a curated selection of 16 events, not a census of all Pokémon media and merchandise.
TCG comparisons
The 2017–2025 calendars use audited but provisional TCGdex expansion candidates. Identified promotions, card subsets, decks and digital TCG Pocket releases were excluded. A set and a distinct launch date are counted separately; source catalogues may be incomplete. The 2023–2024 relationship diagram shows selected major source sets, not a complete card-by-card mapping.
Japan Poké Lid maps
A fixed community-maintained snapshot contains 470 unique recorded lids across 41 of Japan's 47 prefectures. The Generation I map counts each lid once if it features at least one Gen I species. Six prefectures without recorded lids are shown as no data, not zero percent. The snapshot is not a live official installation total.
